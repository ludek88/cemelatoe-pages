What does “camel toe” mean?

Camel toe is slang for the outline of the outer labia through fitted clothing. Here is where the name comes from, what it does not mean, and how to discuss it respectfully.

By Camela Toe Editorial · Published July 23, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026

Camel toe meaning: “Camel toe” describes an outline of the outer labia visible through fitted clothing at the crotch. It refers to the appearance of the fabric over the body, with the clothing still covering it. The phrase is slang and can sound mocking when used about someone else.

The outer labia are part of the vulva, the external genital area. Naming the anatomy makes the definition clearer; it does not imply that the wearer’s body needs correcting. The outline may be noticeable in leggings, swimwear, or other fitted clothes. For clothing construction rather than the definition, use the camel toe guide.

Why is it called camel toe?

Merriam-Webster defines camel toe as an informal noun: a usually unwanted crease in tight-fitting clothing, such as leggings, that outlines the outer labia. The dictionary traces the name to a supposed resemblance to a camel’s split toes and gives 1988 as the first known use of that sense. That is a documented-use date, not proof of who coined the expression.

Dictionary.com enters the one-word form “cameltoe,” also spelled camel toe, as slang for the outline of the vulva in tight, stretchy pants. It dates that written record to 1995–2000 and explains the image as a resemblance to a camel’s foot. The two dictionaries do not agree on a first date. Both agree that the name is a visual comparison, not a technical or medical label.

Cambridge Dictionary describes it as an effect of tight trousers or underwear that makes the outer shape visible. One of its examples is a catsuit, which is a useful reminder that the phrase is not limited to leggings.

The phrase in everyday use

The expression is a visual comparison. Dictionaries classify it as informal language, and people use it in several different ways: to identify a garment-fit issue, to describe a fashion photo, or, less helpfully, to make a joke about another person’s appearance.

The outline itself is ordinary. Stretch fabric, a short garment rise, a central front seam, movement, and camera lighting can all affect how visible it becomes. Two people can wear the same design and get different results. One person can also try two garments marked with the same size and find that only one creates the outline.

What the term does not mean

  • It does not describe a medical condition.
  • It does not indicate that a body is abnormal.
  • It does not prove that clothing is the wrong numerical size.
  • It does not tell us whether the wearer notices or cares.

Garment pattern, seam construction, torso length, fabric weight, and activity can matter as much as the number on a label. That is why “just size up” is not a universal answer. The leggings fit guide compares seams, gussets, and rise. Swimwear behaves differently when it is wet; that comparison is in the swimwear section of the overview.

Is the phrase offensive?

Meaning depends on intent and context. A person may use the term neutrally while shopping for activewear or asking how a seam should fit. Directing it at a stranger, posting an unsolicited close-up, or using it as a punchline can be humiliating and invasive.

A respectful conversation focuses on the garment and the wearer’s own comfort: “This front seam pulls when I move” is more useful than turning someone’s anatomy into commentary. If you are discussing another person, the safest assumption is that their body is not a public problem to solve.

How to describe a fit concern clearly

If you are asking about your own clothing, describe what changes during wear and what you would like to change. These examples keep the conversation specific:

  • In a fitting room: “The front seam pulls upward when I sit. Can I try another cut?” This gives the assistant a fit detail to work with.
  • In a product review: “The waist fits, but I keep adjusting the front after walking.” This describes your experience without claiming that everyone will have the same result.
  • When a friend asks for an outfit check: “Do you want feedback on comfort, coverage or the overall look?” Let their preference guide the answer.

For a stranger who has not asked, there is no need to comment. A visible outline alone does not tell you whether someone is uncomfortable or wants advice.

Body-positive principle: a visible outline is not a wardrobe emergency. Changing it is a styling preference, not an obligation.

Frequently asked questions

What does camel toe mean?

It is informal slang for the outline of the outer labia visible through fitted clothing at the crotch. The clothing still covers the body. The phrase describes the fabric, not a medical condition.

Why is it called camel toe?

The name compares that divided outline with the split toes of a camel’s foot. Merriam-Webster dates a documented use to 1988. Dictionary.com dates the one-word form’s written record to 1995–2000. Neither date identifies who first said it.

Is camel toe a medical term?

No. Dictionaries list it as informal or slang. Pain, numbness, broken skin, or irritation that continues after a clothing change should be discussed with a qualified clinician. This page is not medical advice.

Is it rude to say camel toe?

It can be. A shopper describing their own fit is different from commenting on a stranger’s body. If you were not asked, there is no need to point it out.
