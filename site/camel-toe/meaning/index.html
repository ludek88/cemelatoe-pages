Plain-language definition

What does “camel toe” mean?

A camel toe is the outline of the outer labia that shows through fitted clothing at the crotch. The clothes still cover the body. The phrase is informal slang for that fabric outline. It is not a medical condition, and it is not a judgment about someone’s body.

By Camela Toe Editorial · Published July 23, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026

What it means as slang

Camel toe is informal language for a clothing outline, not a clinical label. Someone may use it to name a fit issue in their own clothes, to describe a photograph, or to joke about another person. The first two can be practical. The joke usually is not, because it treats someone else’s body as the punchline.

The outline can show up in leggings, swimwear, trousers, or any other fitted garment. How that garment is cut is the subject of the clothing guides linked below. This section stays with how people use the words.

Why is it called camel toe?

Merriam-Webster defines camel toe as an informal noun: a usually unwanted crease in women’s tight-fitting clothing, such as leggings, that outlines the outer labia. The dictionary traces the name to a supposed resemblance to the two split toes of a camel and gives 1988 as the first known use of that sense. That is a documented-use date, not proof of who coined the expression.

Dictionary.com labels it slang and defines it as the outline of the vulva as seen when a woman is wearing tight, stretchy pants. It dates that written record to 1995–2000 and explains the image as a resemblance to a camel’s foot. The two dictionaries do not agree on a first date. Both treat the name as a visual comparison, not a technical or medical label.

Cambridge Dictionary describes it as an effect of tight trousers or underwear that makes the outer shape visible. One of its examples is a catsuit, which is a useful reminder that the phrase is not limited to leggings. Those entries are written about women’s clothing. This guide cites them for the name’s history and for the clothing outline in the definition above.

Dictionary.com’s headword is the one-word form cameltoe, and that entry also gives the two-word spelling. The hyphenated spelling camel-toe is the same term; Cambridge’s page address uses it, while the entry itself shows two words. Merriam-Webster’s plural is camel toes: more than one instance of that outline, not a different body part.

What the term does not mean

  • It does not describe a medical condition.
  • It does not indicate that a body is abnormal.
  • It does not prove that clothing is the wrong numerical size.
  • It does not tell us whether the wearer notices or cares.

Pain, numbness, broken skin, or irritation that continues after a clothing change should be discussed with a qualified clinician. This page is not medical advice. Garment pattern, seam construction, torso length, fabric, and activity can matter as much as the number on a label, which is why “just size up” is not a universal answer.

Is it considered rude?

It can be. A person may use the term neutrally while shopping for their own clothes or asking how a seam should sit. Directing it at a stranger, posting an unsolicited close-up, or using it as a punchline can be humiliating. If you were not asked, there is no need to point it out.

A respectful conversation stays with the garment and the wearer’s own comfort. These examples keep it specific:

  • In a fitting room: “The front seam pulls upward when I sit. Can I try another cut?” That gives the assistant a fit detail to work with.
  • In a product review: “The waist fits, but I keep adjusting the front after walking.” That describes one experience without claiming everyone will have the same result.
  • When a friend asks for an outfit check: “Do you want feedback on comfort, coverage, or the overall look?” Let their preference decide whether the outline is even relevant.

For a stranger who has not asked, silence is the respectful choice. A visible outline does not tell you whether someone is uncomfortable or wants advice.

Body-positive principle: a visible outline is not a wardrobe emergency. Changing it is a styling preference, not an obligation.

Why does it happen?

The outline appears when fitted fabric follows the outer labia instead of bridging over them. A few clothing details make that more likely, and they often show up together:

  • Fit and rise. A short rise can pull the front upward even when the waistband feels fine. A long walk or a deep bend makes that pull easier to see than a still pose does.
  • Seams. A center-front seam can draw cloth inward along one line. A gusset and a seamless front are different patterns, and neither one promises the outline will disappear.
  • Thin or wet fabric. A light, shiny, or very stretchy knit shows small contours more than a heavier matte cloth. Wet fabric clings and can become easier to see through, so swimwear can look different in the water than it did in a dry fitting room.

Two garments with the same size on the label can still look different, because the pattern changed rather than the body. The clothing overview explains how those factors combine, including swimwear, lining, and wet fabric. The leggings guide compares a center-front seam with a gusset and a smoother front.

How to avoid it

People also ask how to avoid it. Nothing removes the outline for every body and every garment, and nobody is required to hide a normal contour. If a smoother front is what you want, treat it as a clothing choice:

  • Compare the rise and the front seam before you assume the size is the problem.
  • Feel the fabric. A heavier matte knit often shows less than a thin, shiny one. In swimwear, look at the front lining itself rather than trusting the word “lined” on a hangtag.
  • Sit, walk, and bend while you can still take the garment back. A mirror pose does not show where the panels settle.
  • Skip inserts or extra layers that cause pressure, numbness, or rubbing. Comfort matters more than a perfectly smooth outline.
